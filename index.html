How the calculations work

A percentage of a number

Divide the percentage by 100, then multiply by the number. For example, 15% of 200 is 30. A 15% discount on $200 saves $30.

One number as a percentage of another

Divide the first number by the total, then multiply by 100. For example, 25 out of 200 is 12.5%. The total cannot be zero.

Percentage increase or decrease

Subtract the original value from the new value, divide by the original value, then multiply by 100. Going from 80 to 100 is a 25% increase; going from 100 to 80 is a 20% decrease. The original value cannot be zero. Negative original values can produce results that need extra interpretation.

Results are rounded for display to a maximum of six decimal places. Very small nonzero results use scientific notation so they are not mistaken for zero.

Recover an original amount

Choose “Original amount before an increase” or “Original amount before a decrease.” Enter the Final amount and the nonnegative rate r as a percentage number. Original amount = final amount ÷ (1 + r ÷ 100) before an increase. Original amount = final amount ÷ (1 − r ÷ 100) before a decrease. For example, 150 after a 25% increase came from 120; 80 after a 20% decrease came from 100. Results are amounts without a percent sign or assumed currency.

These two modes require nonnegative amounts and rates. A decrease must be below 100%; a 100% decrease cannot identify a unique original amount from zero, and is inconsistent with a nonzero final amount. Increases above 100% are allowed. Zero rate leaves the amount unchanged. Reverse results outside the finite range or too small to represent without rounding to zero show an error. Keep matching units and treat rounded inputs as approximate. See the original-price guide for worked steps, also available without JavaScript.

Worked percentage examples

Find a sale price

A $200 item is 15% off. Choose “What is X% of Y?” and enter 15 and 200. The result, 30, is the discount, not the final price. Subtract it from $200: $200 − $30 = $170, before any applicable taxes or fees.

Work out a score

You answered 36 of 40 questions correctly. Choose “X is what percent of Y?” and enter 36 and 40. The calculation is 36 ÷ 40 × 100 = 90%. The second number is the total possible score.

Compare an old price with a new price

A price rises from $80 to $100. Choose “Percentage change from X to Y,” with 80 as the original value and 100 as the new value. The $20 increase is divided by the original $80, giving 25%. Reversing the numbers gives −20%, because the starting value is now $100.

Compare percentage rates

A rate moving from 10% to 15% rises by 5 percentage points. Its relative percentage increase is 50%, calculated as (15 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100. Percentage points measure the difference between rates; percentage change measures the difference relative to the starting rate.

Percentage change vs percentage points

When comparing two rates, percentage points describe the subtraction between the rates. Percentage change compares that difference with the original rate. Label the answer: the same movement can have two correct numbers with different meanings.

Percentage-point difference = new rate − original rate. Use the rates as percentage numbers, not fractions. For a nonzero original rate, relative percentage change = (new rate − original rate) ÷ original rate × 100.

A rise from a positive starting rate

Suppose the share of survey respondents choosing an option moves from 10% to 15%. The difference is 15 − 10 = 5 percentage points. Relative to the original rate, (15 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 = 50%. That means the rate is half again as large, not that it rose by 50 percentage points.

A fall from a positive starting rate

If a rate falls from 20% to 15%, the signed difference is 15 − 20 = -5 percentage points, a fall of 5 points. Relative percentage change is (15 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = -25%. The original rate is the denominator; switching the starting and ending values changes the relative answer.

Zero and negative starting rates

From 0% to 5%, the difference is 5 percentage points. Relative percentage change is undefined, not 0% or infinity, because its formula would divide by zero. Report the starting and ending rates and the point difference instead.

For a signed measure such as a reported return moving from -10% to -5%, the difference is 5 percentage points. This calculator divides by the signed original value, so (-5 − (-10)) ÷ (-10) × 100 = -50%. The rate moved upward even though that relative result is negative. With a negative starting value, report both rates and the point difference rather than interpreting the sign as an ordinary increase or decrease.

Check the relative change with this calculator

In the free calculator, choose Percentage change from X to Y. Enter the earlier rate in Original value and the later rate in New value, then select Calculate. Enter percentage numbers without a percent sign: 10 and 15 returns 50%; 20 and 15 returns -25%; -10 and -5 returns -50%. Entering 0 and 5 shows the nonzero-original-value error. This mode does not calculate percentage points; subtract the original rate from the new rate separately.

Compare like with like

Use the same measure, population definition and time basis for both rates. Different sample sizes or groups can limit what the comparison means. A change alone does not demonstrate causation or statistical significance. If source rates were rounded, the calculated difference is approximate; keep available precision and state the source precision when reporting it. These examples illustrate arithmetic, not actual survey or investment performance.

Combine percentages from unequal groups

If you want the percentage across all people or items in several groups, add the relevant counts and divide by the combined total. Simply averaging the group percentages gives each group equal influence, even when one group is much larger. This is an overall proportion, not percentage change between rates.

Combined percentage = sum of matching counts ÷ sum of group totals × 100. When each group's rate is exact and its total is positive, this is also a weighted average of the rates, using the group totals as weights.

Example: two groups completing the same task

In one group, 18 of 20 people complete a task: 90%. In a second group, 40 of 80 complete it: 50%. Together, 18 + 40 = 58 people complete the task out of 20 + 80 = 100, giving 58%. The simple average, (90% + 50%) ÷ 2 = 70%, is not the overall completion rate. It answers a different question: the average of two equally weighted group rates.

The larger group contributes more people, so it should contribute more weight to the overall result. The equivalent weighted calculation is (90 × 20 + 50 × 80) ÷ 100 = 58%. Prefer the original counts when you have them rather than reconstructing counts from rounded percentages.

When an ordinary average works

If the groups have equal totals, averaging their exact rates gives the same pooled rate. For example, 18 of 20 and 10 of 20 are 90% and 50%; together they are 28 of 40, or 70%. Equal percentages can also happen to give the same result with unequal group sizes, but that does not make an unweighted average a reliable general method.

Check the pooled rate in the calculator

First add the matching counts yourself, then add the group totals separately. In the free calculator, choose X is what percent of Y?. Enter the combined count in Part and the combined total in Total. For the unequal-group example, enter 58 and 100 and select Calculate to get 58%. For the equal-group example, enter 28 and 40 to get 70%. Do not enter the two percentages as Part and Total: that compares them instead of pooling their groups.

Check the denominator and the meaning

  • Use the same outcome definition and comparable observation period across groups. Groups must be non-overlapping for this simple addition; otherwise a person or item may be counted twice.
  • For a share of people or items, counts must be nonnegative and matching counts cannot exceed their group totals. Use actual counts rather than treating missing data as zero. Other kinds of ratios can have different domains.
  • If the combined total is zero, the combined percentage is undefined. The calculator rejects Total 0. By contrast, 0 of 20 is 0%: a zero matching count is valid when the total is positive. A genuinely empty group adds no observations; do not average an invented rate for it.
  • If you only have rounded rates or do not know the sample sizes, you cannot in general recover the exact overall rate. Obtain the original counts, or label a calculation from rounded rates as an estimate.
  • Population estimates that use survey weights or a specialist sampling design may need those weights rather than raw group sizes. A pooled rate describes these combined observations; it does not by itself show why groups differ or prove a treatment worked.

Common questions

Which calculation should I choose?

Use the first mode when you know a percentage and want an amount. Use the second when you know a part and a total and want a percentage. Use the third when you want to compare an original value with a new value. Use either original-amount option when you know the final amount and the increase or decrease rate. Keep both amounts in the same units.

What happens when the starting value is zero?

Percentage change from zero is undefined because its formula divides by the starting value. You can still describe the absolute change: from 0 to 20 is an increase of 20 units. Similarly, a part cannot be expressed as a percentage of a zero total.

Can I enter negative values or percentages above 100?

The first three modes allow them. For example, 150% of 20 is 30. Negative values follow the displayed formulas, but percentage change with a negative starting value can be counterintuitive. A change from −10 to −5 gives −50% mathematically, even though the value increased. Consider reporting the absolute change as well. The two original-amount modes instead require nonnegative inputs; increases may exceed 100%, but decreases must be below 100%.

How precise are the results?

Results use browser floating-point arithmetic and are rounded to at most six decimal places for display. Nonzero results with absolute magnitude below 0.000001 use scientific notation with up to seven significant digits: for example, 1E-8 means 0.00000001. Extremely small calculations can still underflow to zero in browser arithmetic. Use appropriate precision and independently check figures before relying on them for important decisions.

Do I need an account to calculate?

No account or download is needed. The calculator runs directly in your browser.

How results are produced

The calculator runs JavaScript in your browser. It multiplies for a percentage of a number, divides for a part-to-total percentage, and divides the difference by the signed original value for percentage change. See the formulas, the percentage-points explanation and the discount guide for worked steps. A zero denominator is rejected; negative starting values require care when interpreting relative change.

It uses browser floating-point numbers, not exact decimal or arbitrary-precision arithmetic. Ordinary results are rounded for display to at most six decimal places. Very small nonzero results use scientific notation with up to seven significant digits. Intermediate arithmetic can lose precision, underflow to zero or exceed the supported range. The display is not a guarantee of exactness; independently check important calculations and currency rounding.
