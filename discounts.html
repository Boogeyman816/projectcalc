Discounts and reverse percentages

A sale label tells you the rate of reduction, not the amount you will pay. Start by identifying the original price, the discount rate and whether another discount applies to an already reduced price. This guide works without JavaScript; the free calculator can help check the steps.

Discount amount vs final price

The discount amount is the money saved. The final price is the original price minus those savings. For an original price P and a discount rate r written as a percentage:

Discount amount = P × r ÷ 100
Final price = P × (1 − r ÷ 100)

Example: 25% off a $120 jacket

The saving is $120 × 25 ÷ 100 = $30. Subtract the saving: $120 − $30 = $90 to pay before tax or fees. The calculator's percentage-of result is the saving, not the checkout total.

In the calculator, choose What is X% of Y?, enter 25 in Percentage (%) and 120 in Number, then select Calculate. Subtract the result, 30, from 120 yourself. The guide links open the calculator but do not prefill its fields.

Recover the original price

If you know the reduced price and the discount rate, divide by the fraction of the price that remains. Adding the same percentage back does not undo a discount: it uses a different starting amount.

Original price = reduced price ÷ (1 − r ÷ 100)

Example: an $80 item after 20% off

The remaining share is 100% − 20% = 80%, or 0.8. The original price is $80 ÷ 0.8 = $100. Check it forward: 20% of $100 is $20, leaving $80. Adding 20% to $80 would give $96, not $100.

Choose Original amount before a decrease and enter 80 and 20: 80 in Final amount and 20 in Decrease rate (%). Select Calculate to get 100, an amount without a percent sign or assumed currency. In this example it is the original price in dollars. Enter the discount rate itself, not the remaining percentage.

Recover an amount before an increase

The same idea works beyond discounts: divide the final amount by its multiplier. For a positive original amount and a nonnegative rate r, an increase uses 1 + r ÷ 100; a decrease uses 1 − r ÷ 100. Choose the direction first, rather than subtracting the percentage from the final amount.

Original amount = final amount ÷ (1 + r ÷ 100)

A container holds 150 litres after its contents increased by 25%. The original amount was 150 ÷ 1.25 = 120 litres. Check forward: 120 × 1.25 = 150. Subtracting 25% from the final amount instead gives 150 × 0.75 = 112.5 litres, which does not undo the increase because it uses the wrong base.

To check this example in the free calculator, choose Original amount before an increase and enter 150 and 25: 150 in Final amount and 25 in Increase rate (%). Select Calculate to get 120, the original amount in litres here, without a percent sign. Enter the increase rate itself; do not add it to 100 first.

At a 100% decrease, the multiplier is zero and the reverse division is undefined. A zero final amount cannot identify a unique original amount; a nonzero final amount is inconsistent with an exact 100% decrease. Decreases above 100% and negative starting amounts are outside this positive-amount interpretation. A 0% change leaves the amount unchanged; an increase can exceed 100% without making its multiplier zero. With rounded inputs, the recovered amount is only approximate. Use a rate relative to the original amount, in matching units, and account separately for any fixed additions or removals.

Successive discounts

When the second discount applies to the reduced price, multiply the remaining fractions. Do not simply add the rates. Check the offer terms: coupons may exclude sale items, apply only to certain products or be capped.

Final price = P × (1 − first rate ÷ 100) × (1 − second rate ÷ 100)

Example: 20% off, then another 10% off a $100 item

The first saving is $20, leaving $80. The second saving is 10% of $80 = $8, leaving $72. Total savings are $28, so the combined discount is 28%, not 30%.

Choose What is X% of Y? and enter 20 and 100. Subtract the result from 100. Stay in that mode and enter 10 and 80, then subtract the result from 80. To check the combined rate, choose X is what percent of Y? with Part 28 and Total 100; it returns 28%.

Limits, rounding and checkout totals

  • Use nonnegative prices in the same currency. Ordinary discounts run from 0% to 100%. A negative rate describes an increase, while a rate above 100% is outside this ordinary sale-price model.
  • At 100% off, the reduced price is zero; it does not identify a unique original price. The reverse formula divides by zero, so use it only for discount rates below 100%.
  • Examples exclude tax, delivery charges and fixed-value coupons. A store may apply these in a different order. Compare the offer terms and receipt, not only the advertised rate.
  • Keep intermediate precision where possible and round the payable amount to the currency's minor unit. For dollar examples, that is normally two decimal places. Retailers may round each discount separately, which can change the final cents.
  • The calculator uses floating-point arithmetic and rounds ordinary displays to at most six decimal places. It is not a currency ledger; very large or tiny values can lose precision or exceed its range. Independently check amounts before a purchase.
